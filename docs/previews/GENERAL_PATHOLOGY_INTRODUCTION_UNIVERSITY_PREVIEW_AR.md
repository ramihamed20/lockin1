General Pathology - Introduction - University Edition
01 / INTRODUCTION TO GENERAL PATHOLOGY
Pathology is the scientific study of structural, biochemical and functional changes in cells, tissues and organs that underlie disease.
General Pathology examines the common cellular and tissue responses to injurious stimuli across all organs.
Systemic Pathology examines the specific responses of specialized organs and organ systems to defined diseases.
Homeostasis is the steady-state equilibrium maintained by normal cells within a narrow physiological range.
The four core aspects of a disease process are Etiology, Pathogenesis, Morphologic Changes and Clinical Manifestations.
Etiology refers to the initiating cause of disease, classified into genetic factors and acquired environmental factors.
Pathogenesis describes the sequence of cellular, biochemical and molecular events from initial stimulus to disease expression.
Morphologic Changes include Gross (macroscopic) and Microscopic (histologic) alterations characteristic of a disease.
Clinical Manifestations represent the functional consequences of morphologic changes, expressed as Symptoms, Signs and Prognosis.

General Pathology - Introduction - University Edition
02 / CELLULAR ADAPTATIONS TO STRESS
Cellular Adaptation is a reversible structural and functional response that allows cells to survive altered physiological demands or pathological stimuli.
Hypertrophy is an increase in the size of cells resulting in an increase in the size and functional mass of the organ, without cell division.
Hypertrophy is characteristic of permanent non-dividing cells such as cardiac myocytes in Left Ventricular Hypertrophy.
Hyperplasia is an increase in the number of cells in an organ or tissue capable of replication, driven by growth factors or stem cell proliferation.
Physiologic Hyperplasia includes Hormonal Hyperplasia (such as breast and uterine epithelium) and Compensatory Hyperplasia (such as liver regeneration).
Atrophy is a decrease in cell size and cell number resulting in reduced tissue or organ mass due to decreased protein synthesis and increased degradation.
Protein degradation in Atrophy occurs mainly via the Ubiquitin-Proteasome Pathway and Autophagy with residual Lipofuscin granules.
Metaplasia is a reversible change in which one differentiated cell type (epithelial or mesenchymal) is replaced by another cell type better suited to stress.
Classic examples of Metaplasia include Squamous Metaplasia in respiratory epithelium of smokers and Columnar Metaplasia in Barrett Esophagus.

General Pathology - Introduction - University Edition
03 / REVERSIBLE AND IRREVERSIBLE CELL INJURY
Cell Injury occurs when environmental stress exceeds the cell’s capacity for Cellular Adaptation or when the stimulus is inherently damaging.
Major causes of Cell Injury include Hypoxia, Ischemia, chemical toxins, infectious pathogens, immunologic reactions and nutritional imbalances.
Ischemia (reduced blood supply) causes faster and more severe tissue injury than Hypoxia alone because glycolysis substrates are also depleted.
Core biochemical mechanisms of Cell Injury include ATP Depletion, Mitochondrial Damage, Calcium Influx, Oxidative Stress and Membrane Permeability defects.
Reactive Oxygen Species (ROS) cause lipid peroxidation of membranes, oxidative modification of proteins and DNA strand breaks.
Reversible Cell Injury is characterized morphologically by Cellular Swelling (Hydropic Change) and Fatty Change (Steatosis).
Cellular Swelling results from failure of the ATP-dependent sodium-potassium plasma membrane pump, leading to intracellular sodium and water accumulation.
Irreversible Cell Injury ensues when severe Mitochondrial dysfunction and plasma or lysosomal membrane rupture pass the point of no return.

General Pathology - Introduction - University Edition
04 / CELL DEATH: NECROSIS VERSUS APOPTOSIS
Necrosis is an unregulated pathological form of cell death resulting from severe membrane damage and enzymatic digestion of leaked cellular contents.
Necrosis always elicits a local host reaction called Inflammation to clear necrotic debris and initiate tissue repair.
Nuclear changes in Necrosis include Pyknosis (nuclear shrinkage and hyperchromasia), Karyorrhexis (nuclear fragmentation) and Karyolysis (chromatin dissolution).
Major tissue patterns of Necrosis include Coagulative Necrosis, Liquefactive Necrosis, Caseous Necrosis, Fat Necrosis and Fibrinoid Necrosis.
Coagulative Necrosis preserves basic tissue architecture for days and is characteristic of Ischemic Infarction in solid organs except the brain.
Apoptosis is a regulated, energy-dependent pathway of programmed cell death mediated by proteolytic enzymes called Caspases.
Apoptosis is triggered through the Intrinsic (Mitochondrial) Pathway regulated by BCL2 family proteins and Cytochrome c, or the Extrinsic (Death Receptor) Pathway.
In Apoptosis, cells shrink and form membrane-bound Apoptotic Bodies that are rapidly cleared by Phagocytosis without plasma membrane rupture or Inflammation.
